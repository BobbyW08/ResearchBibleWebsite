# What Do You Want — Getting the Most From Your First Coaching Session

You're sitting in a parenting coach's office, and you're probably not thrilled about it. Maybe your kid's school called. Maybe your partner insisted. Maybe you're just exhausted and desperate and hoping someone has a magic fix. Whatever brought you here, the first session feels like it matters — because it does. What happens in that first conversation sets the tone for everything that follows. But here's the good news: the session isn't designed to fix anything yet. It's designed to listen, to understand what you actually want (which might be different from the problem you came in with), and to build the kind of working relationship where real change becomes possible.

## Why the First Session Isn't About Solutions Yet

You might expect your first coaching session to feel like this: you describe the problem, the coach explains what's wrong, and they hand you a list of strategies to try. But that's not how it works — and if that's what happened, your coach missed an important step.

Research shows that parents who feel genuinely heard and understood in early sessions stick with coaching and make bigger changes than parents who jump straight to strategies. If you're sitting in that first meeting still feeling like nobody really gets it, or like the coach is just applying a one-size-fits-all approach to your specific mess, you're more likely to:

- Skip the homework they assign
- Miss the next appointment
- Nod along but not actually use what they suggest
- Quit within the first few weeks

The reason is simple: **people are more likely to make changes they actually want than changes someone tells them they should make.** Your coach's job in that first session isn't to tell you what to do. It's to help you figure out what you actually want — which sounds obvious, but most parents haven't thought about it in those terms.

When you came in saying "my kid won't listen" or "I'm at my wit's end," you were describing the problem. But there's a difference between describing a problem and articulating what you want instead. That's what the first session is really about.

## What Readiness Actually Looks Like — And Why It Matters

Before we dive into what a good first session involves, let's talk about where you're actually starting from. Readiness for change isn't binary (ready or not ready). It's more like a spectrum, and your coach is trying to figure out where you land — not to judge you, but to match their approach to where you actually are.

**You might be in "precontemplation" if:** You came here because someone else wanted you to. You don't actually think the problem is yours to fix — it's your kid's problem, or your partner's problem, or the school's problem. You're not really convinced change is necessary. You're there because you had to be.

If this is you, the worst thing a coach can do is launch into behavior strategies and homework. That's like handing someone a gym membership when they don't want to go to the gym. It just makes them feel more resistant.

**You might be in "contemplation" if:** You know something needs to change, but you're stuck. You see the problem, you're worried about it, but you're also uncertain. You don't know if you have the energy. You're not sure it'll work. You're ambivalent — which is completely normal and human.

This is actually a really productive place to be. This is where real exploration can happen.

**You might be in "preparation" if:** You're convinced something needs to change, and you're ready to try. You might have already tried a few things that didn't work. You're looking for what actually will work. You're past the "does this need to happen" question and into the "how do we do this" question.

This is where you're ready to learn specific strategies — but even here, it matters that those strategies connect to what you actually care about.

Your coach isn't going to use those exact terms. But they'll be tuning into which of these matches where you are, and adjusting their approach accordingly. A good coach doesn't treat a parent who's doubting and ambivalent the same way they treat a parent who's ready to roll up their sleeves and try something new.

## Listening First, Talking Later

Here's something that might feel weird in your first session: the coach might talk less than you expected and ask a lot of questions. They might not jump in with information or advice. They might just... listen.

This is actually sophisticated. It's called **reflective listening**, and research shows it's one of the most powerful things a coach can do in an early session.

Here's what reflective listening sounds like:

You say: "I'm just so exhausted. I feel like I do everything, and he just refuses everything I ask."

Coach says (reflecting back): "So underneath all of this, what you're really carrying is that it all falls on you. And nothing you try seems to land with him."

Notice the coach didn't say: "Well, you need to stop asking and start telling." They didn't launch into a behavior explanation. They just fed back what they heard — including the emotion underneath the words.

This does two things: (1) it makes you feel like you were actually heard, and (2) it gives the coach information about what this problem really means to you. Because the *problem* isn't just "my kid won't listen." The *problem* might be "I feel invisible and alone in this." Or "I feel like a failure." Or "I'm terrified something is really wrong."

The coach is trying to find that deeper piece — not because they're nosy, but because *that's* the piece that will actually keep you motivated when things get hard.

## Finding Your "Why" — Values vs. Goals

Let's say your presenting problem is: "My 10-year-old talks back constantly and won't follow directions."

A goal would be: "My son will listen the first time I ask him to do something."

But a value would be something deeper: "I want to feel like a calm, strong parent. I want my son to respect me and to actually want to work with me instead of against me."

Notice the difference? A goal is specific and measurable. A value is about who you want to be and what kind of relationship you want to have.

Both matter. Goals give you something to aim for. But values are what keep you going when it's hard.

Research on behavior change shows that when people connect their goals to deeper values — to what actually matters to them — they stick with change longer and achieve bigger shifts. This is why a good coach will ask questions like:

- "What kind of parent do you want to be?"
- "What do you want your child to remember about their childhood?"
- "If we fix this thing that brought you in, what becomes possible?"
- "When your child is grown, what do you want your relationship to look like?"

These aren't therapy questions. They're practical coaching questions. The answers are what will anchor you through the whole coaching journey.

Your coach might write down your answer — the thing you said that felt true and important. They might even type it up and send it to you after the session. That's your direction statement. It's not a goal yet. It's more like your north star. When you're frustrated three weeks in, that's what you'll come back to.

## The OARS Technique: What a Listening Coach Actually Does

If your coach is well-trained, they'll be using something called OARS in that first session. It's not a mystical thing — it's a framework for how to listen in a way that actually works.

**Open questions** are questions that can't be answered with "yes" or "no." Instead of "Do you think things are bad at home?" they ask "What's the hardest part of things at home right now?" Open questions invite you to tell your story, not just confirm what the coach thinks.

**Affirmations** acknowledge something true and good about you — not fake cheerleading, but genuine recognition. If you've been trying strategy after strategy even though nothing has worked, a coach might say: "You've kept showing up even though you're tired. That says something about how much you care." This sounds simple, but it does something important: it reminds you that you're not a failure. You're a person who cares and keeps trying.

**Reflective listening** is what we talked about above — feeding back what you said, including the feeling underneath it.

**Summaries** pull everything together. The coach might say: "Okay, let me make sure I've got this. You're dealing with a lot of morning chaos, and you feel like you're the only one who cares about being on time. Your kid seems to have no idea that it matters. And underneath all that, what you want is for things to feel a little calmer and for your kid to understand how their behavior affects you." Then they stop and let you correct them if they got something wrong.

The point of OARS isn't to manipulate you. It's to make sure the coach actually understands what you're dealing with before they start suggesting what to do about it.

## What Doesn't Belong in Your First Session

Here's what a good first coaching session does NOT include (or at least, doesn't do first):

**Long intake forms.** Some coaches want to fill out a detailed form right at the start. But filling out a form is weird when you're trying to build a relationship. A good coach will either do the intake stuff before the session or after they've had a chance to actually talk with you.

**Lots of psychoeducation.** You don't need to hear about brain development or behavior theory or attachment science in your first session. That's not because it's not useful — it is — but it's useful *after* you feel understood, not instead of feeling understood. If your coach is lecturing you, you're not building the relationship you need.

**A list of strategies.** "Here's what you need to do: one, two, three, four, five." Nope. Not yet. You need to be ready to actually try these things, and readiness takes a conversation, not a handout.

**Focusing only on your child.** If the conversation never gets to *your* experience, what you need, what you're struggling with, then you're getting incomplete coaching. The child's behavior is part of the picture, but how the parent is doing and what the parent needs is the other part.

## What Actually Happens: A Real-Looking First Session

Let's walk through what this might look like in practice. You're sitting in your coach's office, nervous or skeptical or exhausted or all three.

**The first few minutes:** Your coach greets you, maybe offers water, and makes small talk. They're trying to help you feel safe. Then they probably say something like: "I know you didn't necessarily choose to be here, and I want to start by asking what brought you in today. What's been going on?"

They're not asking for the intake summary. They're asking for your story.

**The next 15–20 minutes:** You tell them what's happening. Your kid won't listen, or you're at wit's end, or school called, or whatever it is. As you talk, your coach is doing a lot of listening and reflecting. They might ask follow-up questions like "What does that look like when it's at its worst?" or "How long has it been like this?" But mostly they're listening.

They're NOT saying: "Oh, I see. That's oppositional defiant disorder" or "Classic anxiety response" or "You need to use more positive reinforcement." They're listening.

**20–30 minutes in:** Your coach probably starts reflecting back some of what they heard, including the feelings underneath it. "It sounds like you're carrying a lot, and you're frustrated because you're trying but nothing seems to work." They might ask you about things you've already tried. They're mapping the territory.

**30–40 minutes in (if this is going well):** Your coach might start asking some of the bigger questions. "When you imagine things being different at home, what does that look like?" or "What kind of parent do you want to be?" or "What's most important to you about your relationship with your kid?"

These questions sometimes surprise parents because they're not what you expected. But they're important. The answers are what will anchor everything else.

**Last 10–15 minutes:** You and your coach might identify one small thing you'll focus on or think about before the next session. Not a homework assignment (not yet), but maybe a direction. "I'm hearing that what really matters to you is that your daughter feels safe coming to you with hard stuff. That's a really important value. Before we meet again, could you just notice one moment when she does come to you with something? Just notice it. You don't have to do anything differently yet. Just notice."

At the end, your coach probably summarizes what they heard and why you're coming back. They might email you a brief recap. They've established the beginning of a working relationship.

That's it. No magic fixes. No 10-step plan. Just listening, understanding, and a sense of direction.

## When Something's Not Working in That First Session

Good first sessions don't always feel comfortable. You might cry. You might get frustrated. You might feel resistant. That's all normal.

But there are some signs that something's off:

**You don't feel heard.** The coach keeps redirecting to their framework instead of understanding yours. They're not asking questions about your experience — they're telling you what you need to know.

**You feel blamed.** A good coach might raise the idea that parenting patterns could shift, but they do it with curiosity, not judgment. If you're leaving feeling like the coach thinks you're doing everything wrong, that's not a good fit.

**You're getting advice before you've had a chance to think out loud.** If by 15 minutes in the coach is already telling you what to do, it's too fast.

**Your kid's experience is the only thing being talked about.** You matter. Your stress, your history, what you need — that matters to your ability to show up as a parent.

**The coach isn't asking about context.** Culture, resources, family structure, other stressors — these all affect what's realistic. If your coach isn't curious about the real context of your life, their suggestions probably won't fit.

If you're noticing these things, you can say something. "I don't think this is the right fit" is a complete sentence. But it's also worth giving it one more session to see if there was just a mismatch in that first one.

## Real Scenarios: What Your Coach Might Be Thinking

**Scenario 1: You come in and immediately minimize the problem.**

You say: "It's really not that bad. I don't even know why I'm here. My partner thinks it's a bigger deal than it is."

What your coach is thinking: *This parent isn't convinced change is necessary yet. They're in precontemplation. If I jump to strategies now, they'll nod, leave, and never use anything. Instead, I need to explore a little. Is there anything that bothers you? Even something small?*

Your coach might say: "It sounds like you're here partly because your partner thought you should come. That makes sense. But I'm curious — is there anything about how things are at home that frustrates you, even a little? Even something small?"

They're not trying to convince you there's a problem. They're looking for the tiniest bit of openness so you're not walking out of here completely unmotivated.

**Scenario 2: You come in and you've tried everything and you're demoralized.**

You say: "We've tried three programs, medication, therapy — nothing works. I don't know why I'm here. Nothing ever changes."

What your coach is thinking: *This parent has worked hard but lost hope. They need their sense of agency restored. I need to acknowledge how much they've tried and also help them see that they're still showing up, which means some part of them hasn't given up.*

Your coach might say: "You've done a lot of work. You haven't given up even though that would be easier. That tells me something. What keeps you coming back? What keeps you trying?"

They're not dismissing your exhaustion. They're trying to find the part of you that hasn't lost hope completely, because that's the part that will carry you forward.

**Scenario 3: You're ambivalent and your reasons for change and reasons against change are equally balanced.**

You say: "I know things need to be different. But I also don't know if I have it in me. I'm already doing so much. I don't know if I can add more."

What your coach is thinking: *This is the perfect place to work. This parent sees the need for change and is just wrestling with whether they can actually do it. I should help them think about both sides without pushing.*

Your coach might say: "So you want things to be different, and you're also realistic about how full your plate already is. Both of those things are true. Tell me more about what you're worried would happen if you tried something new. What would be hard?"

They're not trying to convince you to change. They're inviting you to think out loud about what's really stopping you — because usually when you think out loud about it, you start figuring out whether it's actually stopping you or whether it's just scary.

## FAQ: What Parents Usually Want to Know About First Sessions

**Q: Do I have to commit to a whole program right in the first session?**

A: No. The first session is a conversation, not a contract. You should leave knowing whether you want to continue, but you're not locked in.

**Q: What if I'm nervous or I cry?**

A: That's completely normal and fine. Your coach has seen nervous and tears. It's not a problem.

**Q: Should I prepare something to say?**

A: You don't need to. But if you have a specific question or concern, it's okay to write it down so you don't forget it.

**Q: What if I disagree with the coach about what the problem is?**

A: Say so. "I feel like you're not understanding what I meant" is a fair thing to say in session. A good coach will want to know.

**Q: How long does a first session usually take?**

A: Usually 50–60 minutes, depending on the coach's model.

**Q: What if the coach asks me about stuff I wasn't expecting?**

A: They might ask about your own childhood, your stress, your support system, your hopes. These aren't random — they're trying to understand the full picture of what's happening.

**Q: Can my partner come to the first session?**

A: Sometimes yes, sometimes the coach prefers to meet with you alone first. It's okay to ask what works best for your situation.

**Q: What if I'm not sure I'll actually follow through on anything?**

A: That's honest. It's also worth exploring in the first session — "What do I need in order to actually use what we talk about?" — because if you don't have the answer yet, you will by the end of a good first session.

## Key Takeaways: What a Good First Session Does

- **Makes you feel genuinely heard.** Not just listened to, but actually understood. Your coach can reflect back to you what they heard in a way that feels accurate.

- **Surfaces what you actually want, not just what you're complaining about.** There's a difference between "my kid needs to listen better" and "I want to be a calm parent who my kid actually wants to work with." A good coach finds that second piece.

- **Doesn't rush to solutions.** If you leave the first session with a full action plan and you don't feel ready, something was missed.

- **Creates a working relationship where you believe the coach gets it.** You might not like everything they suggest, but you trust they understand your actual situation.

- **Identifies your readiness level and adjusts accordingly.** A coach who treats someone who's reluctant exactly the same as someone who's ready to go all-in is missing the mark.

- **Gives you a direction to return to.** Ideally, you walk out knowing what matters most to you about this change — the "why" underneath the "what."

- **Doesn't shame you.** Not once. Not subtly. Never. If you're feeling blamed, that's a sign something's off.

## When to Seek Additional Help

A good parenting coach is trained and competent. But if any of these things come up in your first session, additional support might be needed:

**If you're in crisis:** An immediate safety concern with your child or in your home isn't something coaching alone addresses. Your coach should help you access crisis support.

**If domestic violence is happening:** This isn't a parenting coaching problem. This is a safety problem that needs specialized support before parenting goals can be set.

**If you're having suicidal thoughts:** This needs mental health crisis support, not parenting coaching. Your coach should know how to connect you to that.

**If you've experienced trauma:** Parenting coaching can help, but if you're noticing that the conversation about your child is triggering difficult feelings about your own childhood or past experiences, mental health support alongside coaching (or first) might be important.

**If you have severe depression or anxiety:** Coaching can work alongside mental health treatment, but if your depression or anxiety is severe enough that you can't engage in a conversation about change, mental health treatment should come first.

**If you're using substances in a way that's affecting your parenting:** This isn't shame — this is reality. A coach might help, but substance-specific support is usually necessary too.

A good coach will recognize these situations and help point you toward the right support. They won't pretend they can handle everything.

## Sources & Evidence Base

**Therapeutic alliance predicts outcomes.** Hawley and Weisz (2005) found that the working relationship between parent and coach is one of the strongest predictors of whether a child will actually get better. Norcross and Lambert (2011) showed this effect holds across almost every type of treatment. A good first session builds that alliance.

**Dropout is highest in the first two sessions.** Nock and Kazdin (2001) and Chacko et al. (2016) document that 20–60% of families drop out of parenting programs, and most of that dropout happens within the first two to three sessions. What happens in those early meetings matters.

**Readiness for change is a real thing.** Prochaska and DiClemente (1983) mapped out stages of change. Not everyone comes in ready to change. Lavi et al. (2025) found that assessing and matching readiness level actually improves coaching outcomes.

**Motivational Interviewing works.** Miller and Rollnick (2013) developed MI, which has now been tested in over 1,200 studies. It's one of the most-researched approaches to building motivation for change. Hettema and Hendricks (2010) found it improves completion rates and actual skill use in parent training.

**Values matter more than goals.** Hayes et al. (2011) showed that connecting behavior change to deeper values increases both motivation and long-term success. Locke and Latham (2002) found that goals without underlying meaning are abandoned.

**Practitioner responsiveness is key.** Lindsey et al. (2014) did a meta-analysis of what makes early engagement work. The answer: coaches who actually adapt to the specific person in front of them, rather than using a one-size-fits-all approach.

**Prior experience affects readiness.** Ingoldsby (2010) found that families who've been through ineffective programs before need even more careful relationship-building in the first session — they're not skeptical for nothing.
