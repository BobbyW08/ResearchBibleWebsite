# Big Transitions, Big Feelings — Helping Kids Through Life Changes

When your family goes through a major transition — moving to a new house, welcoming a new sibling, starting a new school, or navigating a divorce — your child's world shifts. What felt predictable suddenly becomes confusing. And kids don't always tell us how they're feeling. Instead, they might have meltdowns, regress to baby behaviors, get aggressive, or withdraw. Many parents wonder: Is this normal? Am I handling it right? This guide will help you understand what's happening inside your child's brain and give you concrete tools to support them through change.

---

## What's a Transition? Why It Matters to Your Child

A major life transition is any big change that disrupts your child's normal routine and predictable world. Moves. New siblings. School changes. Parental divorce. Even positive changes — like moving to a bigger house or starting an activity your child wanted — are transitions.

Transitions matter because kids need two things to feel okay: they need to feel secure with their parent, and they need to know what to expect in their environment. Major transitions shake both of these foundations. Suddenly, the parent is busy with logistics or stress. Suddenly, what was familiar is gone. Your child's nervous system senses this disruption as a threat, even when the change is ultimately positive.

This doesn't mean your child is broken or overly sensitive. It means their brain is working exactly the way brains are supposed to work — by noticing when safety markers change and calling for help.

---

## Why Kids Struggle with Transitions: The Neuroscience

Your child's brain is still developing the parts that manage emotions and stress. These abilities — staying calm when things change, comforting themselves, thinking clearly through worry — aren't fully baked until the mid-20s. Until then, your child borrows regulation from you. When you're calm and predictable, your child's nervous system learns to calm down too.

Transitions break this system in two ways:

**First, they activate threat detection.** Your child's brain picks up on uncertainty ("What will happen?") and treats it like a threat. The part of the brain that detects danger — the amygdala — starts firing. This is why kids can't just "think it through" or "use logic." Their survival brain is in charge.

**Second, they often take you offline as a regulator.** If you're stressed about moving, grieving a divorce, or managing a newborn, you're less available to calm your child. Your child senses this. They notice you're distracted. Your calm presence — the thing they need most — is harder to access. This creates a loop: child gets more upset → parent gets more stressed → parent is less available → child gets even more upset.

**The power of predictability.** Here's the good news: research shows that knowing what will happen is one of the most powerful ways to calm a nervous system. Kids who are told in advance about changes, given concrete details, and know what to expect show better adjustment than kids who experience surprises. Your brain can handle hard things; uncertainty is what breaks us.

## Understanding Regression: The Behavior You'll See

When kids go through transitions, they often act younger than their age. A toilet-trained preschooler might have accidents. A kindergartener who used to sleep alone suddenly needs to come to your bed. A second-grader who reads fluently wants you to read to them like you did in preschool. Or a school-age child uses baby talk.

Here's what's happening: under stress, your child's brain narrows to survival mode. They revert to behaviors that used to make them feel safe — behaviors that got them care and closeness when they were younger. This isn't manipulation. It's not a sign that you've done something wrong. It's a stress signal.

**How to respond:** Regression tells you your child is overwhelmed and needs extra connection right now, not correction. If you respond with frustration ("You're too big for that"), shame ("Why are you acting like a baby?"), or punishment, your child gets a double message: "I'm struggling AND I'm bad for struggling." Instead, meet regression with warmth. Let them use a bottle for a few weeks if they need to. Read to them even if they can decode the words. Your child will grow back to their age-appropriate abilities once the transition settles. This phase is temporary.

## Common Transitions and What They Feel Like for Kids

### Moving to a New Home

A move affects every system in your child's life at once: the physical space, the routines, the social connections, the school, the neighborhood. Kids often experience this as losing everything familiar at the same time. They feel this loss even if the new home is "nicer" or the neighborhood is "better."

**What kids might show:** sadness and tears after school or at bedtime; complaints about the new place; reluctance to explore or engage; complaints of stomach aches or headaches; difficulty sleeping in a new bed; increased clinginess.

**How long adjustment takes:** Most kids begin to settle within 3–6 months of a single move. But middle-school-age kids (around 10–14) have a harder time because friends are becoming central to their identity. This is the toughest move age.

### Welcoming a New Sibling

The birth of a new sibling is one of the most common major transitions in early childhood. Overnight, your older child loses exclusive access to you — the person who regulated them, paid attention to them, and made them feel special. This is a real loss, even if intellectually your child wanted a sibling.

**What kids might show:** increased aggression (toward the baby or siblings), behavioral regression, attention-seeking behavior, clinginess, jealousy, or withdrawal. Some kids show increased tantrums in the weeks right after the baby arrives.

**What research shows:** Most kids don't experience long-term harm from a sibling's birth. The transition is harder when the older child has a difficult temperament, when the parent is overwhelmed or depressed, or when the parent pulls back warmth and connection while managing the newborn.

### Starting a New School

School transitions — kindergarten entry, moving schools mid-year, the jump to middle school, or the transition to high school — predictably stress kids. The biggest stumble happens when kids go from elementary to middle school. In middle school, kids have multiple teachers, bigger peer groups, and more social complexity at the exact moment they're most sensitive to peer belonging.

**What kids might show:** anxiety or complaints about the new school; reluctance to go; complaints of stomach aches; difficulty sleeping; increased anxiety about social situations; clinginess after school.

**What helps most:** visiting the new school before it starts; knowing one friendly person in the new environment; maintaining home routines while school changes; and teachers who take time to know kids personally.

### Parental Divorce or Separation

Divorce is a transition that restructures your child's entire world. Your child loses the predictability of living with both parents. There may be new houses, new rules, new routines, changes in seeing one parent. And often, kids are in the middle of parental conflict.

**What kids might show:** sadness, anger, or numbness; confusion ("Is this my fault?"); behavioral changes (aggression, withdrawal, school problems); physical symptoms (headaches, stomach aches); resistance to visiting the other parent; anxiety about the future.

**What research tells us:** About 75–80% of kids ultimately do okay after divorce. The factor that matters most isn't the divorce itself — it's whether your child is exposed to ongoing parental conflict and whether both parents stay involved in their lives. Kids do better in a divorced family with low conflict than in an intact family with high conflict.

## The Behavior Interpretation Loop: What You Might Be Thinking vs. What's Actually Happening

Here's a common moment:

| What You See | What You Might Think | What's Actually Happening | What Helps |
|---|---|---|---|
| Your 6-year-old wets the bed after the move. | "She's regressing. I need to put her back in pull-ups to show her this isn't okay." | She's overwhelmed and has lost the predictability that allowed her body to manage this skill. Her brain is in survival mode. | Respond with warmth, not consequences. Extra comfort at bedtime. A waterproof mattress pad. This will resolve as she settles. |
| Your newly-big-sister clings to you while you're trying to feed the baby. | "She's being clingy and demanding attention. I need to set a boundary so she learns the baby comes first." | She's lost exclusive access to her primary regulator (you) and is panicking. The clinging is a stress signal. | Give her scheduled 1:1 time with you every single day. Let her be close while you feed the baby. Her brain needs to trust you haven't left. |
| Your 13-year-old after the move won't come to family dinner, won't talk, just stays in their room. | "They're being disrespectful. They need to learn they're part of this family." | They're grieving the loss of friends and identity, and they're self-protecting by withdrawing. Forcing connection will push them further away. | Stop demanding engagement. Instead, create low-pressure opportunities (car rides, sitting next to them, movie nights). Prioritize reconnecting with old friends. Find a new activity that matches their interests. |
| Your child has a meltdown about school refusing to go after the family divorce. | "They're manipulating me. If I let them stay home, they're learning they can avoid school." | They're genuinely frightened and dysregulated. School feels unsafe because their world has shifted and school is now one more unknown. | Validate the fear ("I know this feels scary"). Don't force. Work with the school on a reentry plan. Consider if the school counselor can be a check-in point. Build gradually. |

**The key insight:** These behaviors are communication from a dysregulated child, not defiance or manipulation. When you respond to the child's need instead of the behavior, the behavior usually shifts on its own.

## The PACE-ACT Model: Your Framework for Guiding Kids Through Transitions

Here's a practical model that works across all types of transitions:

### **P — Prepare (Before the Transition)**

Tell your child what is coming. Use concrete language and simple explanations matched to their age. Don't just tell them what's changing — tell them what stays the same.

Instead of: "We're moving to a new house."
Try: "We're moving to a new house on Maple Street. Your bedroom will have a blue door. We'll keep our family dinner on Friday nights. Grandma still lives two blocks away. You'll go to a new school with a blue playground."

**Why it works:** Predictability calms the amygdala. Your child's brain can prepare when it knows what's coming.

### **A — Acknowledge Feelings (Before, During, and After)**

Let your child feel their feelings. Don't try to fix or minimize them. Don't reassure too quickly. Instead, reflect back what they're feeling.

Instead of: "Don't worry, you'll love the new school!"
Try: "I can see you're really sad about leaving your old school. That makes sense. You had good friends there."

**Why it works:** When feelings are validated, they don't get stuck. Kids who are told their feelings are wrong often feel ashamed and keep the distress internal. Kids who feel understood can actually move through the feeling.

### **C — Continuity (During and After)**

Keep at least one or two important things the same. Pick the most important routine or ritual and protect it.

Maybe it's: family dinner at 6 PM, bedtime stories, Saturday movie nights, Sunday breakfast at your favorite spot, or a weekly call with Grandpa.

**Why it works:** When everything changes at once, kids lose their sense of anchor and safety. One predictable thing signals "the family is still here, we're still us."

### **E — Empower (During and After)**

Give your child real choices within the transition.

For a move: "You can pick the color for your new room and choose how to arrange your furniture."
For a new sibling: "You can help pick out baby clothes and decide what to teach the baby."
For a school transition: "You can choose one activity you want to do this year."

**Why it works:** Helplessness amplifies anxiety. Agency — even small agency — builds confidence and reduces the sense of "this is being done to me."

### **A — Attune (Throughout)**

Pay attention to how your child is actually doing. Kids don't always tell you they're struggling. Look for changes in: mood, sleep, appetite, school performance, social withdrawal, increased aggression, physical complaints (stomachaches, headaches), or regression.

Check in regularly but gently, without interrogating. "I noticed you seemed quiet today. What was that about?" is better than "Why aren't you talking to me?"

**Why it works:** Problems are easier to address early. You can't help with something you don't know about.

### **C — Connect First, Then Redirect (Throughout)**

When your child is dysregulated — having a meltdown, being aggressive, withdrawn, or acting out — the first step is connection, not correction.

Before you redirect behavior, acknowledge what your child is experiencing: "You're really upset. I'm right here. Let's sit for a minute." Once your child is calmer (this might take minutes or hours), then you can problem-solve or redirect.

**Why it works:** A dysregulated nervous system can't access learning. Connection — physical closeness, your calm presence, validation — lowers cortisol (the stress hormone) and allows the thinking brain to come back online. Then the child can hear you.

### **T — Time and Professional Threshold (After)**

Know what normal looks like so you don't panic or stay complacent.

- Most kids show significant adjustment within **3–6 months** of a single move
- Most kids begin to stabilize within **3–4 months** after a new sibling arrives
- School adjustment often takes the full **first semester**
- Divorce adjustment typically takes **6–12 months**, sometimes longer

If your child isn't progressing within these timelines, or if symptoms worsen instead of improve, it's time to seek professional support. That's not failure — that's good parenting.

## Before the Transition: Concrete Preparation Strategies

### Tell Them Early

The amount of time to prepare depends on age:
- **Preschoolers (3–6):** A few weeks in advance is appropriate
- **School-age (6–12):** A few months works well
- **Teens:** Longer, if possible, and with more involvement in planning

### Use Concrete Language, Not Just Abstract Concepts

Instead of "We're getting a new sibling," try: "Mom and Dad decided we're going to have a baby. The baby will grow in Mom's tummy for about 9 months. When the baby is born, they'll cry a lot, and we'll all need to help take care of them. Your job is still to be a big sibling, but also you'll still have special time with each of us."

### Include Them in Preparation

- **For a move:** Let them pack their own box, pick their room color, see photos of the new house
- **For a new sibling:** Help set up the baby's room, pick out items, feel the baby kick
- **For a school change:** Visit the new school, meet the teacher if possible, find one friend in advance
- **For a divorce:** Both parents deliver the news using consistent language, answer questions, and provide reassurance

### Create a Transition Object or Ritual

A stuffed animal that travels with them. A photo of your old house. A journal where they draw their feelings. A goodbye ritual for the old space. These portable symbols of continuity help anchor kids through change.

### Familiarization Visits

Visit the new school before the first day. Walk through the building, show them where the classroom is, where the bathroom is, where they'll eat lunch. Do this multiple times if possible. For a move, spend time in the new house, in the new neighborhood.

Research on school transitions shows that kids who visit in advance have lower first-day anxiety.

## During the Transition: Staying Close

### Protect Connection Time

This is critical. When everything is chaotic — during a move, managing a newborn, or navigating divorce logistics — kids need MORE connection with you, not less.

Even 10 minutes of undivided 1:1 time per child per parent makes a difference. Put the phone away. Play a game. Go for a walk. Sit together quietly.

### Maintain Key Routines

You don't have to keep everything the same. Pick one or two things and hold them:
- Bedtime routine (same stories, same songs, same tucking-in)
- Family dinner time
- Weekend breakfast tradition
- Bedtime cuddles

These signaling moments tell your child: "We are still us."

### Name Emotions Out Loud

"I can see you're sad right now." "This is scary and that's okay." "You're angry about leaving your friends, and that makes sense."

This does two things: it validates their experience, and it teaches them that emotions are observable and manageable, not shameful.

### Don't Take Regression Personally

Your toilet-trained 4-year-old has accidents after the move. Your independent 7-year-old suddenly needs you to sleep in their room. This isn't a failure on your part. It's their nervous system asking for extra scaffolding while it recalibrates. Respond with warmth, not frustration.

### Stay Calm and Regulated

Your child is watching you and reading your nervous system. If you're anxious, scattered, or dysregulated, they absorb that. You don't have to be perfect or pretend everything is easy. But try to be grounded when you're with them. Take breaks if you need to. Breathe. Your regulation is their regulation.

## After the Transition: What to Expect and How Long It Takes

Different transitions have different timelines:

### After a Move
- **First month:** Kids often seem fine or even excited, then hit a wall. This is when sadness and grief surface.
- **Months 2–3:** The hardest phase. School feels hard. They miss old friends. They don't know new friends yet. The new room doesn't feel like home.
- **Months 3–6:** Gradual integration. They're making connections at school. The new place starts to feel familiar. Routines re-establish.
- **6+ months:** Most kids are settling in and building new attachments.

**During this time:** Don't minimize their sadness. Allow video calls with old friends. Don't force them to "love" the new place. Find activities that match their interests in the new community. Be patient.

### After a New Sibling
- **First weeks:** Older siblings might seem welcoming, then behavioral problems emerge as the reality of sharing you sinks in.
- **First 3 months:** Peak adjustment difficulty. Regression, aggression, or withdrawal are common.
- **Months 3–6:** As parents find a rhythm with the new baby and protect 1:1 time with the older child, older sibling's behavior often improves significantly.

**During this time:** Protect 1:1 time with the older child every single day. Avoid shameful comments about acting younger. Give the older child "jobs" helping with the baby (not as punishment, but as meaningful contribution). When possible, have one parent be the primary responder to the older child while the other manages the baby.

### After Changing Schools
- **Before day one:** Anxiety peaks.
- **First weeks:** Kids often come home exhausted. Social anxiety is normal. "I don't know anyone" is expected.
- **First month:** They're mapping the social landscape and finding where they fit.
- **First semester:** Real friendships begin forming.
- **By spring:** Most kids have found their people and the new school starts to feel normal.

**During this time:** Don't push friendship formation. Let it happen organically. Arrange one playdate with a classmate early on. Have them identify one adult in the building (teacher, counselor, coach) they feel safe with. Keep home routines solid.

### After Parental Divorce
- **Immediately:** Kids are in shock or making meaning out of what happened ("Is this my fault?" "Do both of you still love me?").
- **First 3 months:** Reality sets in. Kids process grief, anger, fear about the future.
- **First 6 months:** Kids are adjusting to logistics (two houses, new routines, changed schedules).
- **6–12 months:** Emotional integration deepens. Kids develop a "new normal."

**During this time:** Be consistent with communication in both households. Protect kids from parental conflict. Don't use kids as messengers or informants. Don't speak negatively about the other parent. Kids adjust better when they see you both managing the change maturely.

## Different Transitions, Different Needs

### Moves During Middle School (The Toughest Age)

Moves hit hardest between ages 10–14 because friendships have become central to identity. A child losing their peer group is losing a core part of how they see themselves.

**What helps:** Validate that this is genuinely harder than moving at other ages. Don't minimize with "You'll make new friends." Do help them maintain old friendships (video calls, visits) and actively support new friendships (extracurricular activities in areas matching prior interests). Stay patient. This adjustment might take 6+ months.

### New Sibling and Parental Postpartum Depression

Parental mood affects child adjustment dramatically. A mother struggling with postpartum depression is less available to the firstborn exactly when the firstborn needs extra connection. This amplifies the firstborn's distress.

**What helps:** If you're struggling after a new baby, please ask for help and consider mental health support. This isn't failure — it's essential. Your child's adjustment depends on your wellbeing.

### Divorce and High Ongoing Conflict

Kids manage divorce reasonably well if they're protected from parental conflict. But if they're witnessing or being caught in the middle of conflict, this creates independent harm.

**What helps:** Establish a co-parenting communication protocol that doesn't involve the child. Use a parenting app or third party for logistics. Shield kids from adult conflict. If you and your ex can't communicate without conflict, consider co-parenting coaching or family law support.

### Multiple Simultaneous Transitions

Sometimes transitions pile up: a move coinciding with a school change, or a new sibling and parental separation. Multiple transitions at once are harder than single transitions.

**What helps:** If possible, space transitions out. If you can't, provide extra support, more connection time, and lower expectations for behavior. This is a high-demand time for your child.

## When Kids Need Extra Support: Special Considerations

### Kids with Autism Spectrum Disorder

Autistic children's need for predictability and routine isn't a preference — it's neurological. Changes to routine can create significant distress (meltdowns, shutdowns, aggression).

**What helps:** Prepare extensively. Use social stories explaining the new environment. Make multiple visits to new spaces. Use visual supports showing the new sequence. Involve familiar support people or therapists in the transition if possible.

### Kids with ADHD

ADHD involves reduced executive function — the ability to organize, plan, and manage change. Major transitions overwhelm this system.

**What helps:** Create highly structured transition planning. Use written schedules and visual supports. Break the transition into smaller steps. Provide consistent adult guidance.

### Kids with Anxiety Disorders

Anxious kids may show anticipatory anxiety long before the transition and avoidance behavior (refusing school, somatic complaints) as it approaches.

**What helps:** Graduated exposure (visiting the new school or place multiple times). Coping skills practice. Validating the anxiety while also gently pushing them to face the feared situation.

### Kids Who've Experienced Trauma

Children with developmental trauma have a nervous system primed to perceive threat. Novelty and uncertainty feel dangerous. Transitions can reactivate trauma responses: hypervigilance, aggression, dissociation, or collapse.

**What helps:** Extra co-regulatory support. High predictability. Coordination with a trauma-informed clinician. These kids need more scaffolding through transitions.

### Parents in Crisis

Here's a hard truth: your adjustment predicts your child's adjustment. A parent who is grief-stricken after divorce, overwhelmed by a newborn, or stressed by a move has reduced capacity to co-regulate the child. And children sense this.

**What helps:** This is an investment, not an indulgence: get support for yourself. Therapy, coaching, community, practical help with logistics. Taking care of your mental health is taking care of your child's mental health.

## When to Seek Professional Help

Normal transition adjustment includes some distress, regression, or behavioral changes. But some signs mean it's time to bring in professional support.

**Talk to a professional if:**

- **Duration:** Your child shows significant distress that doesn't begin to improve within 3–6 months of a move or 6–12 months after divorce
- **Severity:** Your child has acute functional impairment (can't attend school, stops eating, stops grooming, has no peer relationships) at any point
- **Self-harm:** Your child expresses not wanting to live, has thoughts of self-harm, or is engaging in self-injurious behavior
- **Dissociation:** Your child appears zoned out, disconnected from reality, or reports not remembering events
- **Ongoing high conflict (divorce):** Parents can't communicate without conflict, and your child is being triangulated into the conflict
- **Physical symptoms:** Persistent headaches, stomachaches, or other physical symptoms that don't respond to typical support

Seeking professional support isn't failure. It means you're recognizing that your child needs specialized help to process a major life change.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Transitions disrupt the two things kids need most: your secure presence and a predictable environment.** Understanding this shift how you respond.

2. **Regression, aggression, withdrawal, and anxiety during transitions are stress signals, not character flaws.** These behaviors are your child's way of saying "I'm overwhelmed."

3. **The single best predictor of how well your child adjusts is how well you adjust.** Protect your own mental health and regulation.

4. **Preparation, predictability, and continuity are your most powerful tools.** Telling kids in advance what will happen, maintaining one key routine, and staying emotionally present make a real difference.

5. **Connection comes before correction.** When your child is dysregulated, your calm presence and validation matter more than behavioral consequences.

6. **Most kids adjust well to major transitions with support.** 75–80% of kids do okay after divorce. Most kids settle within 3–6 months after a move. Your child can do this.

7. **Transitions build resilience when they're navigated with adequate support.** You're not protecting your child from all difficulty (impossible and counterproductive). You're teaching them that hard things can be managed with connection and preparation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: How much detail should I tell my young child about an upcoming move/new sibling/divorce?**

A: Enough to be honest and reduce uncertainty, but not so much that they're worried about adult concerns. "We're moving to a new house" is enough for a 4-year-old. You can add details like room color and that they'll still see Grandma. You don't need to discuss the reason for a move or financial stress. The goal is truthfulness plus predictability, matched to their age.

**Q: My child is acting out more during the transition. Does this mean they're worse at handling change?**

A: No. Behavioral escalation during transitions is normal. Some kids show feelings. Some kids show behavior. A child who is aggressive or defiant might actually be a child whose feelings are very big and who doesn't have words for them yet. This is a chance to teach them those words and help them regulate.

**Q: Should I try to keep everything the same as it was before the transition?**

A: You can't, and you shouldn't try. Transitions involve real changes. But keeping one or two anchor routines signals that the core family unit is intact. Pick one and protect it.

**Q: My child says they hate the new place/school/situation. Should I take this literally?**

A: Not necessarily. Kids often express big transitional grief as "I hate it." This doesn't mean they'll always hate it. Validate the feeling ("I hear that you're really unhappy right now") without letting it dictate next steps. Kids' feelings about transitions shift as they settle.

**Q: When should I worry that my child isn't adjusting normally?**

A: Within 3–6 months of a move or school change, and 6–12 months after divorce, most kids begin to show meaningful improvement. If your child's distress is worsening instead of improving, or if there's no shift after these timelines, that's a good time to check in with a professional. Also watch for: inability to attend school, not eating, not sleeping, expressing hopelessness, or physical symptoms that don't resolve.

**Q: What if I'm not handling the transition well? My stress is affecting my kids.**

A: You're self-aware, which is half the battle. Reach out for support for yourself. A therapist, a trusted friend, parenting coaching, or community support can help you manage your stress so you have more capacity for your child. This is not an indulgence — it's essential.

**Q: How do I talk to my child about divorce without putting them in the middle?**

A: Both parents deliver the news together using consistent language. You might say: "We've made a decision to stop being married. This is not your fault. We both still love you very much. You will be taken care of. Both of us will still be your parents." Then answer their questions honestly. Don't ask them to carry messages or information between households. Don't speak badly about the other parent. Kids do best when they don't feel caught.

**Q: My older child regressed when the baby was born and it's been 6 months. Is this normal?**

A: Most regression resolves within 3–4 months when supported. Six months in and still significant regression suggests your older child may need extra support or that something else is going on (maybe parental depression, maybe fewer 1:1 moments than the child needs). This is a good time to check in with someone — a pediatrician, therapist, or parenting coach.

## Sources & Evidence Base

This guide is grounded in attachment theory, developmental psychology, and research on child resilience through transitions. Here are the key research findings supporting the guidance:

**On why transitions are hard:** Bowlby's attachment theory shows that children use their parents as a secure base and safe haven. Transitions disrupt both. Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory explains that moves and major changes affect children across multiple systems simultaneously.

**On parental adjustment predicting child outcome:** Research across moves, new siblings, and divorce consistently shows that how well the parent copes predicts how well the child copes — often more powerfully than any child-specific factor.

**On advance preparation:** Research on school transitions and moves shows that children who receive explanation and preparation adjust better than those who experience surprise.

**On regression being normal:** Studies on behavioral responses to stress show that regression to earlier developmental behaviors under acute stress is neurologically expected and does not predict long-term maladjustment when parents respond with warmth.

**On residential moves:** Classic research by Wood and colleagues found significant negative effects of family relocation on child growth, development, school function, and behavior. However, most children show meaningful adjustment within 3–6 months. Middle-school-age children (10–14) are most vulnerable due to peer-attachment centrality at this developmental stage.

**On new siblings:** Research from developmental psychopathology perspectives shows that while some children react with increased aggression immediately following a sibling's birth, this subsides in most cases within months. The transition is harder when parental stress is high or when parenting quality to the older child decreases significantly.

**On school transitions:** Systematic reviews show that school transition interventions produce modest but consistent benefits, with strongest evidence for social skills training, teacher relationship-building, and family involvement.

**On divorce:** Meta-analyses show that 75–80% of children demonstrate adequate long-term adjustment following divorce. The strongest predictor of poor outcomes is ongoing parental conflict, not the separation itself. Economic hardship and reduced parenting quality account for most of the variance in child outcomes.

**On evidence-based programs:** The New Beginnings Program for divorced mothers shows significant and lasting effects on child mental health, academic achievement, and parent-child relationship quality at 6- and 15-year follow-up.

**On routines and rituals:** Research on naturally occurring family routines and rituals consistently identifies them as protective factors during adversity.

For more detailed information and citations, please refer to the full research bible: *Big Transitions Big Feelings — Moves, New Siblings, Schools, Life Changes.*
